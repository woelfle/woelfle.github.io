# Persona: The Deepening Engineer

## Snapshot

**Name:** Jonas Becker
**Role:** Senior Software Engineer (or strong mid-level on the promotion track)
**Business:** Product company (20-100 engineers), often a mid-size or scale-up
**Experience:** 6-9 years in software, mostly backend/full-stack
**Team:** 4-8 engineers; he reviews peers' code, mentors juniors, owns 1-2 modules end to end
**Tech Savviness:** High — comfortable with the stack, curious beyond it; heavy daily AI user
**Reading Behavior:** Subscribes via RSS, reads on the side — commutes, evenings, the odd slow afternoon; takes notes and collects mental models; shares posts he found valuable

---

## Core Reality of Their Job

Jonas ships. Most of his week is features, bug fixes, code review, and the occasional design conversation where he notices he is out of his depth. He owns modules and mentors juniors — enough responsibility to feel real, not enough to feel like he has arrived. There is a senior title on his horizon, and a gap he can feel between his day-to-day work and the way the principal engineers on his team reason about systems.

That gap is his motivation and his anxiety at once. He can make things work; what he wants is to understand how experienced engineers decide what *right* looks like — where boundaries go, why complexity is worth it here and not there, how to think about a system as a system rather than a list of tickets. He has no mentor to learn this from directly, so he reads, deliberately and with intent.

He also works with an AI on his shoulder. It writes most of his boilerplate and some of his code, and it makes him productive in ways that feel great — and quietly unsettling. He keeps a private worry that the level of skill he has today is exactly the level AI commoditizes first.

Jonas is not thinking about software as an abstract craft. He is thinking: *am I on the right trajectory? Is what I'm learning the thing that still matters in five years? And who is going to teach me the thinking I can't get from a code completion?*

## Hopes & Aspirations

What Jonas *wants* — even if he doesn't always say it:

### Operational

- To make design decisions he can defend with structure, not just instinct.
- To draw good boundaries, name complexity honestly, and design systems that survive their first year.

### Financial

- The senior promotion — and the senior+ trajectory after it.
- To be worth more as AI does more: expertise, not execution.

### Reputation

- To be the engineer others bring their design questions to.
- To be known for thinking, not just for shipping.

### Personal

- To close the gap to the level he admires, without a mentor in the building.
- To feel calm about AI's effect on his career, instead of quietly uneasy.
- To enjoy the craft again — the side projects, the learning — rather than running a treadmill.

## Key Concerns & Fears

These are the emotional landmines a piece of content must avoid triggering:

### The Gap to Senior

- "I can ship anything. I don't yet know when I *shouldn't*."
- "I watch the principal ask one question and the whole design changes. I want to know how they know to ask it."
- "I'm doing the work of a senior, but I don't have the senior's reasoning. The promotion would expose that."

### AI Anxiety

- "The model writes my code faster than I do. If my value is writing code, I'm already obsolete."
- "I worry the level I'm at today is the first level to be automated."
- "I use AI all day and I'm not sure I'm learning anything anymore."

### No Mentor

- "There's nobody at my company teaching me how to think about systems. I'm expected to just pick it up."
- "I read a lot, but reading isn't the same as someone challenging my reasoning."

### No Authority to Fix

- "I see the complexity, the wrong boundaries, the technical debt — and I don't have the voice or the authority to stop it."
- "I raised the concern once. Nobody acted. I don't know how to make it land."

These are the moments where a post *clicks* emotionally:

### Pain-Based Triggers

- "We're about to add a service to fix a problem a module could solve, and I can't articulate why that's wrong."

### Aspiration-Based Triggers

- "I want a learning system that actually compounds, not a feed I scroll."

### Relief Triggers

- "That's the reasoning I was missing — I can use this on Monday."
- "Someone finally named the part of being senior that isn't about the title."

## How They Evaluate Content (Decision Criteria)

Jonas reads with intent. A post earns his attention when:

### 1. Shows the Reasoning (Top Priority)

- Does it show *why* the author decided something, not just what they decided?
- He wants to see the thinking — the trade-off, the test, the moment of doubt.

> "I can find the answer myself. I read to see how someone who's better than me thought it through."

### 2. Reachable Depth

- Does it speak above his current level without losing him?
- Not a beginner's primer, not a survey — a window into how experienced engineers think.

### 3. Takeaway Models

- Is there a framework, test, or mental model he can extract and reuse?
- He collects these the way others collect tools.

### 4. Honest About Difficulty

- Does it admit the hard parts — the costs, the trade-offs, the things that only experience teaches?
- Polished advice that ignores the mess loses him.

### 5. Speaks to His Trajectory

- Does it help him grow toward the role he wants — architect, lead, principal?
- Posts that model senior *judgment* earn loyalty.

## Reading & Discovery Process (How He Actually Engages)

1. **Triggered by a live situation** — a design review he couldn't win, a promotion conversation, a nagging AI thought.
2. **Finds the post via RSS, Hacker News, LinkedIn, or a colleague's share.**
3. **Reads fully, in a way others don't** — he takes notes, saves the post, quotes the good lines.
4. **Tests it immediately** — he applies the model to his own codebase or re-plays his last design discussion through it.
5. **Shares it** — with the one colleague he debates design with, or in the team channel with a "this".
6. **Returns to it** — months later, when he finally faces the situation the post was about, he looks it up again.

## How to Position the Blog to Win

Speak in **thinking, not steps**:

- "The questions the senior engineer asks before anyone writes a line."
- "How to argue a boundary decision when you don't have authority — yet."
- "Why 'make it work' and 'make it right' are different skills, and how to build the second."

Avoid:

- Tutorials and how-tos he can get anywhere.
- Gatekeeping seniority as a secret club — he wants the door opened.
- Hand-wringing about AI without a concrete stance on what he should do about it.
