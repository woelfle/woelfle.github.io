# Persona: The Agile Coach

## Snapshot

**Name:** Anja Hoffmann
**Role:** Senior Agile Coach / Scrum Master (traits fit both, plus internal change agents)
**Business:** Mid-to-large product or enterprise org (100-5,000 people), often mid-transformation
**Experience:** 10+ years in software orgs, 5+ in coaching/process roles
**Team:** Coaches 3-8 teams plus their managers; no line authority over anyone
**Tech Savviness:** Medium — understands engineering well enough to follow and be credible, but is a process/people person by trade
**Reading Behavior:** Reads via RSS and shared links; reads for material — questions, frameworks, case studies she can reuse; forwards posts to managers she wants to influence

---

## Core Reality of Their Job

Anja's week is facilitation and influence. Ceremonies, retrospectives, workshops, one-on-ones with teams and their managers — always with the same underlying task: getting people to change how they work. She runs the retrospectives that surface the real problems, and then she faces the harder part: getting anyone with power to act on them. She has deep expertise and zero formal authority; her leverage is trust, timing, and the right framing.

She is stuck between two realities that lie to each other. Management wants the transformation on a timeline and a slide deck; teams want their pain acknowledged. Anja is fluent in both languages and is frequently the only one who is. She watches frameworks get applied mechanically while the values drain out, and she watches metrics get gamed while the system stays the same — and she is the person who sees it and cannot stop it.

Anja is not thinking about sprint boards. She is thinking about power, flow, and change: who actually owns a decision, what the incentive really rewards, why the org drifts back to its old shape every time. She reads to validate that her observations are shared by serious thinkers outside her bubble, and to collect the ideas and language that make her influence land.

## Hopes & Aspirations

What Anja _wants_ — even if she doesn't always say it:

### Operational

- To move teams from ceremony to actual continuous improvement.
- To find coaching questions and framings that shift a conversation without authority.

### Financial

- To demonstrate that the way of working — not the framework — is where the value is, so the coaching role itself survives budget cuts.

### Reputation

- To be seen as a change agent, not a process babysitter.
- To be credible enough with engineers that they invite her reasoning, not just her ceremonies.

### Personal

- To work on the system, not just the sprint — org design, flow, and leverage.
- To grow beyond agile into the broader craft of systems and change thinking.
- To stop carrying the frustration of knowing what's wrong and lacking the power to fix it.

## Key Concerns & Fears

These are the emotional landmines a piece of content must avoid triggering:

### Process Babysitter

- "I'm measured by ceremonies I don't believe in and blamed for outcomes I don't own."
- "I've become the person who reminds adults to do standup. That was not the job I signed up for."

### Dogma Over Values

- "The org adopted the framework and abandoned the values on the same day."
- "Every time I question a ceremony, I'm told 'that's not Scrum.' The framework became a wall, not a door."

### Lip-Service Org

- "Management wants the transformation to happen to the teams. Never to them."
- "The change initiative is a slide deck. When I ask about the incentive system, the meeting ends."

### Metric Gaming

- "Velocity went up and so did nothing else. We trained the board, not the team."
- "Every metric we add, someone learns to game it before it teaches us anything."

## How They Evaluate Content (Decision Criteria)

Anja reads as a practitioner who needs material she can reuse today. She trusts a post when:

### 1. Useable in Coaching (Top Priority)

- Can she turn it into a workshop exercise, a coaching question, or a framing for a conversation?
- She extracts questions and models, not inspiration.

> "A post that gives me one question to ask a team is worth more than a thesis on agility."

### 2. Anti-Dogma

- Does it treat process as a servant of outcomes — and question the sacred cows?
- She is allergic to recycled agile lore; she wants the principle, not the ceremony.

### 3. Org Realism

- Does it acknowledge power, politics, and incentives — the parts of change no framework solves?
- Posts that pretend process is the whole story lose her instantly.

### 4. Fresh Angle

- Is there a framing she hasn't already heard at every conference?
- Systems-thinking and org-design angles delight her because they go deeper than the framework.

### 5. Engineering Credibility

- Does it help her understand the engineering world she coaches, so her advice lands with engineers?

## Reading & Discovery Process (How She Actually Engages)

1. **Triggered by a coaching situation** — a team stuck, a manager resisting, a transformation going sideways.
2. **Finds the post via RSS, LinkedIn, or a forward from a coach peer or an engineer she trusts.**
3. **Reads looking for material** — she reads with a highlighter for questions, frameworks, and case studies.
4. **Adapts it immediately** — a model becomes a workshop exercise; a question becomes a retrospective prompt.
5. **Forwards it strategically** — to the manager she needs to move, with a one-line framing that names the org problem.
6. **Cites it in coaching** — "let's try thinking about it this way," using the post's framing as neutral ground.

---

## How to Position the Blog to Win

Speak in **systems, incentives, and change**, not framework advocacy:

- "Why Scrum splits power — and what to do when the ceremony hides it."
- "The metric that improved and the system that didn't: how to stop gaming."
- "Project thinking vs. product thinking — the org change that actually matters."

Avoid:

- Agile cheerleading or framework evangelism.
- Advice that ignores power, politics, and incentives.
- Prescriptions for coaches that pretend they have authority they don't have.
