> "Any intelligent fool can make things bigger and more complex. It takes a touch of genius — and a lot of courage — to move in the opposite direction." — Albert Einstein

Every engineer carries a title that says "senior." Fewer carry what the title means. The difference is not years, and it is not the number of technologies in the toolbox. It is where the thinking happens.

<!--more-->

## Problem Domain versus Solution Domain

A mid-level engineer lives mostly in the solution domain: the framework, the service, the code. A senior lives in the problem domain: the customer's actual situation, the constraints that are real, the ones that are imagined.

For a senior, a solution is an *experiment*. When the solution turns out complicated, the senior does not accept the complexity as the price of entry. They go back to the problem and try to simplify *it*. Mid-level thinking assumes the problem is fixed and the solution must grow to fit. Senior thinking asks whether the problem itself is the thing that should shrink.

## Time Windows

Mid-level thinking operates in short windows: the sprint, the feature, the ticket. Senior thinking stretches the window — a year, a decade. Changing a default value is trivial this month; the decision it bakes in will be paid for every month for the next ten years. The length of the window in which you think largely determines the quality of the decisions you make.

## "Good Enough" Is Sufficient

Perfectionism is a junior's luxury and a senior's trap. A senior knows *good enough* is sufficient — not out of sloppiness, but because they can see what the next problem is and where the time should go. The courage Einstein mentions is precisely this: refusing the elegant, complex solution that signals competence, in favor of the boring, simple one that ships.

## Cook versus Chef

There is a difference between knowing *that* and knowing *how* — what Nassim Taleb calls **chauffeur knowledge** and real knowledge. A cook follows the recipe and can reproduce it. A chef understands the chemistry and can throw the recipe away when it stops serving the goal.

Mid-level engineers often have chauffeur knowledge: they have been given the map and can drive it well. A senior has driven so many routes that they can read the terrain itself. Time is a *necessary* condition for this — you cannot shortcut the miles — but it is not sufficient. Years of the same two routes, repeated, still produce a driver, not a cartographer.
