The image that changed my understanding of change management: an organization is a river, and change is not about *stopping* the river and rebuilding the valley. It is about *redirecting* the river — a little at a time, at the points where the flow is weakest. The water was there before you arrived, and it will be there after you leave.

<!--more-->

## The River Was There Before You

Every change initiative starts against a river that took years to carve. The current practices, the power dynamics, the informal networks, the daily rhythm of work — these are not obstacles to the change. They *are* the change's material. An organization's present behavior is the accumulated result of thousands of small decisions, and it has enormous momentum.

Prof. Peter Kruse, the complexity researcher, taught that systems do not change by being pushed in one piece. They change through resonance — the amplification of existing dynamics at the right points. The *Architecture of Complexity* literature says the same thing: you cannot command a complex system; you can only perturb it and watch how the perturbation propagates.

## Two Laws That Govern the River

Two principles from physics keep appearing whenever I write about change, because they always apply:

**The first law of cybernetics (Ashby):** a controller must have as much variety as the system it controls. A change program that has fewer levers than the system has degrees of freedom will fail — not through bad intentions, but through insufficient variety.

**The second law of thermodynamics:** systems tend toward disorder without continuous energy. The change you introduce will decay back to the old state unless you keep feeding it. The redirect is not a one-time act; it is continuous maintenance of the new channel.

## Redirecting Instead of Damning

The practical difference between dam and redirect:

- **Dam:** announce the change, impose it, expect the river to obey. The result is predictable: pressure builds, the dam is respected in the project plan, and the water finds a way around — usually in the form of the old behavior returning quietly.
- **Redirect:** find where the flow is already moving in the right direction and widen that channel. Reduce the resistance where the river is trying to go where you want. Work with the current, not against it.

The techniques of redirect are the same as the techniques of rivers:

1. **Find the existing currents.** What do people already want to do that your change supports? That is where to dig.
2. **Open small channels.** Pilot the change where it fits, and let success — not authority — pull more water into the new channel.
3. **Keep the energy flowing.** The redirect decays. Reinforcement is not a launch event; it is a permanent part of running the organization.

## It Is Always the Same

Kruse's systems, the architecture of complexity, the first law, the second law — they all describe the same reality: you cannot push a system into a new state. You can only redirect the forces that are already there. Change management is river engineering. The water was there before you arrived, and it is not going anywhere.
