There is a concept in German that has no good English equivalent: *Fehlbesetzung* — a misassignment, a person placed in a role whose demands are exactly the opposite of their talents. The historian John Röhl called Kaiser Wilhelm II the most brilliant *Fehlbesetzung* in history. The word is worth recovering, because software organizations produce such misassignments constantly.

<!--more-->

## The Case of Wilhelm II

Wilhelm II did not lack talent. He was intelligent, energetic, ambitious, and eloquent. He was, by most accounts, genuinely gifted at communication and at mobilizing attention. His problem was not ability. It was the *direction* of his abilities: he was placed in a role that demanded restraint, steady judgment, and the discipline to delegate — the precise qualities his temperament lacked.

His brilliance was deployed in the service of self-doubt: the louder he performed, the more he needed the applause to confirm his position. And a system that concentrates decision power in one person turns a mismatch into a national catastrophe. The talents were real; the seat was wrong; the combination was historically destructive.

## The Mechanism of Misassignment

The pattern repeats in every organization, at every scale, and it has a recognizable mechanism:

- **Selection rewards the visible.** Charisma, eloquence, and confidence are selected for — they are what performance, interviews, and promotions can see. Steadiness, judgment, and the willingness to defer are invisible qualities, selected against.
- **The seat amplifies the mismatch.** A wrong assignment does not stay small. The role's demands are daily, and the mis-fit produces daily friction — in a technical lead, a manager, a product owner, the cost compounds.
- **The system protects the mismatch.** By the time the misassignment is visible, the person is established, and correcting it is expensive. So it is deferred, and the deferred cost is paid by everyone around the seat.

{{< alert >}}
**The seat test.**

Before placing someone in a leadership seat, ask not *"are they talented?"* but *"is their talent the talent this seat demands?"* The two questions diverge far more often than selection processes admit.
{{< /alert >}}

## What This Teaches About Selection

The lesson is not about any individual — it is about the selection system:

1. **Define the seat before choosing the person.** Write down what the role actually demands — the temperament, not just the resume. The resume gets people into seats; the temperament either fits or destroys.
2. **Beware the qualities that got them noticed.** The visible strengths that earned the promotion are often not the strengths the seat needs. Separate *what earned the seat* from *what the seat requires*.
3. **Correct misassignments early and kindly.** The most humane thing for everyone is to move someone to a seat where their real talents fit — early, while the damage and the stigma are small. Delaying protects no one.

The tragedy of the *Fehlbesetzung* is not that the person was untalented. It is that the talents were wasted — and that the organization chose, for years, to keep them in the seat that wasted them. Selection is not a reward for talent. It is a system for matching talent to demand. Get the matching wrong, and no amount of individual brilliance fixes it.
