Every quarter, teams sit down to write OKRs, and every quarter the room goes quiet. Not because the tool is unfamiliar — because OKRs are hard. The hard part is not filling in the fields. It is that OKRs force you to *decide*: what matters, what you will measure, and what you will *not* do.

<!--more-->

## "Thank God It's Monday"

The test of a real objective is emotional. A genuine objective produces a specific feeling on Sunday evening: not dread of the work, but pull toward the mission. "Thank God It's Monday" is the culture sign for that pull — the organization's people are aligned on something that matters, and Monday is where they get to move it.

You cannot fake this into an OKR. If the objective is bureaucratic, the Sunday feeling is dread. The objective-writing session is really a session about *why anyone should get out of bed* — and that is hard because it is a genuine question, not a template field.

## Outcome versus Output

The second hard part: OKRs are supposed to be outcomes, and everything we are trained to write is output.

- **Output**: "ship the new reporting module."
- **Outcome**: "reduce the time to answer a customer data question from days to hours."

Outputs are comfortable because they are controllable — you can commit to shipping the module. Outcomes are not fully controllable; you can only influence them. And yet the outcome is what the organization actually pays for. Writing an outcome-based key result means accepting that you might do everything right and still miss the number. That is the honest version, and it is hard to write and hard to defend.

{{< alert >}}
**The output dodge.**

"The team shipped 12 features" is a diary, not an OKR. Ask what changed for the user because those features exist. If you cannot name it, the objective was a work plan wearing a costume.
{{< /alert >}}

## Making Decisions

The deepest reason OKRs are hard: a real objective is a decision *not* to do most things. The song every quarter should be, as the famous line goes: *"Tell me what you want, what you really, really want."*

An organization that writes five objectives and fifteen key results has not decided anything — it has listed everything, and listed-everything is the same as prioritized-nothing. The pain of dropping a good idea is not a bug in the system. It is the price of having a direction at all. If writing OKRs does not hurt a little, they were not OKRs.

## The Discipline in Three Moves

1. **Choose one outcome per team that matters most this quarter** — and defend it against everything else.
2. **State the outcome, not the activities** — and accept the loss of control that comes with it.
3. **Check the Sunday feeling** — if the objective does not pull anyone, rewrite it until it does.

OKRs are hard because thinking is hard. The difficulty is not a flaw to be smoothed away. It is the work itself.
