Compared to traditional project management, Scrum's decision to split power within the team into a **Product Owner** and a **Scrum Master** was extremely wise. It is tempting to save a headcount and let one person do both. Tempting — and a quiet disaster.

<!--more-->

## Two Kinds of Questions

A team needs answers to two very different questions:

1. **What** are we building, and for whom — the scope, the priorities, the outcome?
2. **How** are we working — the process, the impediments, the collaboration?

Traditional project management collapses both into one role: the project manager owns scope, schedule, and process. That concentration of power has a structural problem. The process question is always the one that loses.

## The Conflict of Interest

Put yourself in the shoes of a single person holding both roles. A sprint is running behind. The *what* screams: squeeze the team, ship the scope. The *how* whispers: the estimation is broken, the dependencies are rotting, the team is exhausted and needs a retro that actually changes something.

When one person owns both answers, the *how* loses every time. Delivery pressure is loud, present, and concrete. Process improvement is quiet, slow, and long-term. The role of "protect the team's ability to improve" collapses under the role of "make the deadline."

{{< alert >}}
**The result is theater.**

With one person in both roles, the retrospective becomes a formality. Impediments are discussed and scheduled — into the next sprint, where the same delivery pressure waits. Nobody is structurally empowered to say *"we will ship less this sprint so we can get better."*
{{< /alert >}}

## Splitting Power on Purpose

Scrum's answer is deliberate: give the *what* to the Product Owner and the *how* to the Scrum Master, so no single person holds both. The PO is accountable for value and prioritization. The SM is accountable for the process and the team's effectiveness — and is explicitly not the PO's subordinate.

The split is not about headcount or ceremony. It is about making sure the process question always has a seat at the table, even when delivery pressure tries to remove it. Two roles, two accountabilities, one team — and the second question can no longer be silently silenced by the first.
