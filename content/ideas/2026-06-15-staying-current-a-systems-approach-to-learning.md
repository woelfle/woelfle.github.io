Software engineering changes fast, and "staying current" feels like an endless race. But treating learning as a *system* — with inputs, feedback loops, and maintenance — turns it from a panic-driven scramble into something sustainable. This post is my playbook.

<!--more-->

## The Inputs: Curate, Do Not Consume

The internet will happily deliver unlimited content. A learning system starts with a filter, because attention is the scarce resource, not content.

- Follow a small set of high-signal sources, and prune them quarterly.
- Prefer primary sources: papers, RFCs, and release notes over hot takes about them.
- Read books instead of blog posts for foundational topics; books still carry the depth.
- Keep a reading queue with a **cap** — when it overflows, trim it.

{{< alert >}}
**My rule of thirds.**

Spend roughly a third of learning time on fundamentals (still stable after ten years), a third on the stack you use daily, and a third on adjacent or unfamiliar territory. The third is what keeps you current without making you shallow.
{{< /alert >}}

## The Feedback Loop: Learn by Doing

Consuming is not learning. The system only closes when you do something with the material and observe the result:

1. **Apply**: build something small, or refactor an existing piece of code.
2. **Teach**: explain it in a blog post, a talk, or a conversation — teaching exposes the gaps.
3. **Revisit**: come back to the topic weeks later; what seemed hard is now a checkpoint.

A personal blog is a perfect output for this loop. The act of writing turns fuzzy intuitions into precise statements, and readers surface the holes.

## Spaced Repetition: Beating the Forgetting Curve

Human memory decays along an exponential-ish curve. Cramming postpones the decay; spaced review flattens it. The practical version needs no special tools:

- Keep a short list of topics you are learning and note the date you last touched each.
- Review each one after a day, then a week, then a month.
- A decade-old bug, revisited monthly, becomes a case study rather than a faded memory.

The mechanism matters more than the app. Any system that makes you *retrieve* knowledge on a schedule will do.

## Deliberate Practice: The Uncomfortable Kind

Not all practice is equal. **Deliberate practice** means working at the edge of your ability with immediate feedback — not repeating what you already know. For an engineer, that is:

- Rewriting a hot path in a language you are learning.
- Taking on a performance investigation outside your specialty.
- Presenting a design and taking the criticism.

The uncomfortable part is the point. If practice feels easy, the loop is running in place.

## The Maintenance Loop

Knowledge rots. APIs deprecate, best practices move, and your own notes drift out of date. Treat the knowledge base like a codebase:

- Rewrite old notes when you find them wrong.
- Delete notes that no longer matter.
- Link notes so revisiting one surfaces its neighbors.

This is a digital garden: it is maintained by tending, not by planting once.

## Measure the System, Not the Hours

Tracking hours spent reading is vanity. Track outcomes instead: how many things did you *ship* this quarter that you could not have shipped last quarter? How many of last year's opinions survive contact with this year's evidence? Those are the metrics of a learning system that actually works.
