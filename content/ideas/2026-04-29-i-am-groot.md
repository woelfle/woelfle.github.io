"I am Groot." Depending on tone, it means *hello*, *thank you*, *help me*, or *I will destroy you all*. Groot has exactly one vocabulary word, and it is remarkable how much meaning he manages with it — and how much ambiguity he is stuck with. Every engineering team that has exactly one tool, one pattern, or one answer is a Groot team.

<!--more-->

## The One-Tool Team

Some teams organize everything around a single tool or pattern:

- **Microservices** — every problem becomes a service boundary, whether it has one or not.
- **Data products** — every dataset becomes a "product," with ceremony and ownership where a table would do.
- **SCRUM** — every piece of work is a sprint item, every improvement a backlog story, every question a sprint-planning question.

The tool itself may be excellent. That is not the problem. The problem is that a single lens makes some problems *invisible* — not because they are unsolvable, but because the tool cannot express them.

## If You Can See Only One Color

The blindness is not metaphorical. A system that only knows one solution shape does not even *see* the other shapes:

- The team that only knows event-driven architecture sees every coupling as a topic to publish, and never notices the call that should just be a function call.
- The team that only knows "data product" applies ownership and contracts where a shared table was fine — and pays product overhead for trivial needs.
- The team that only knows Scrum runs the ceremony on maintenance, on small fixes, on things that were never projects.

The tool stops being a tool and becomes a worldview. And a worldview cannot be debugged, because the worldview does not know what it is missing.

{{< alert >}}
**The one-question diagnostic.**

Next time your team proposes a solution, ask: *"What would we do if this tool did not exist?"* The answer, spoken out loud, is often the solution the tool could not see.
{{< /alert >}}

## The Full Vocabulary

The fix is not to abandon the favorite tool. It is to grow the vocabulary — to the point where the choice of approach is a *choice*:

1. **Learn the shapes of other tools deeply enough to choose between them.** A team that has built a monolith and a set of services, run batch and streams, used Scrum and Kanban, can *select*. A team with one experience can only *apply*.
2. **Name the trade-off per decision.** The maturity is not in knowing many tools; it is in being able to say *why not* the usual one for this particular problem.
3. **Suspect consensus.** When the whole team nods at the obvious answer, that is not confidence — it is a shared blind spot confirming itself.

Groot got by because his friends translated for him. Your team will not always have that luxury. The color-blind team does not need to apologize for seeing one color — it needs to develop the eyes for the rest.
