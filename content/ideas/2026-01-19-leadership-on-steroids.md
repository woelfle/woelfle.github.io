Every framework in modern software — Scrum, OKRs, architecture reviews, incident processes — is a system of expectations. Sprints are expectations about a slice of work. Key results are expectations about outcomes. Architecture is an expectation about the future shape of the system. And *someone* has to set those expectations. That someone is the leader.

<!--more-->

## Expectations Are the Operating System

Here is the claim, stated as plainly as I can: **setting goals and expectations is the most important task of a leader.** Not hiring, not budgeting, not even strategy in the abstract. Everything a team does is downstream of what it believes it is expected to deliver, and it cannot align to expectations that were never stated.

This is why the same frameworks fail under one leader and sing under another. Scrum works when the Product Owner states a crisp goal and the team knows what "done" means. It collapses into ceremony when the goal is vague and the team improvises priorities in the daily. The framework is the same; the expectation-setting is not.

## Why This Scales

Individual leaders can get away with implicit expectations — they personally correct deviations. The moment you have more than a few people, that stops working. Expectations become the coordination mechanism that replaces the leader's personal attention:

- **Scrum**: the sprint goal aligns six people on what matters this week.
- **OKRs**: the key results align forty people on what matters this quarter.
- **Architecture**: the constraints align many teams on a shape none of them would choose alone.

Setting clear expectations is what turns "do your best" — a meaningless instruction in a complex system — into "achieve this, this way, by then."

## The Part That Is Usually Missing

The hard part is not writing expectations. It is the discipline that surrounds them:

1. **Say them out loud.** An expectation that lives in the leader's head does not exist for the team.
2. **Make them checkable.** An expectation that cannot be tested is a wish, and wishes do not coordinate people.
3. **Rank them.** A team given ten equal priorities has zero priorities.
4. **Repeat them.** Expectation-setting is not a quarter's opening kickoff; it is a weekly, almost daily, act.

A leader who does this well is sometimes described as "leadership on steroids" — not because they shout or dominate, but because they make the whole system move in one direction simply by making the expectations unmistakable. Everything else is follow-through.
