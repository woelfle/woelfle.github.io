I still remember my first Zettel. I had been collecting vocabulary — concepts, terms, definitions — the way the app suggested. Then I read something that rearranged the whole enterprise: a note should encapsulate a **thought**, and thoughts are not words. Thoughts are statements, or questions.

<!--more-->

## A Zettel Is Not a Concept

A concept is a noun: *feedback loop*, *cognitive load*, *servant leadership*. A thought is a claim: *"Feedback loops with long delays cause systems to overshoot."* Or a question: *"Why do teams optimize for the metric they are measured on?"*

If you fill your notes with nouns, you have built an ontology — a taxonomy of labels. It looks organized and it is useless, because knowledge does not live in labels. It lives in the relationships between ideas, and relationships are only expressible in sentences.

## The Web of Thoughts

The Zettelkasten is therefore not an ontology of concepts. It is a **web of thoughts**. Its value is not in the individual notes but in the links between them — each link is an argument, a qualification, or a question connecting one claim to another.

Consider the difference:

| Ontology of concepts | Web of thoughts |
| --- | --- |
| Hierarchy of terms | Network of claims |
| Organizes by category | Connects by argument |
| Fixed structure | Grows organically |
| You browse it | You follow threads |

A concept note asks: *where does this belong?* A thought note asks: *what follows from this?* The second question is the one that produces insight.

## Writing as Thinking

The discipline has a side effect that matters more than the system itself: to write a thought down as a sentence, you have to actually have one. Vague intentions ("I should read more about systems thinking") cannot be stated precisely. The act of phrasing the note forces the thinking — which is the real point of the whole method.
